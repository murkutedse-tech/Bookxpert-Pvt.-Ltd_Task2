# Sleep and Human Health

## Why We Sleep
Sleep is a natural, recurring state in which consciousness is reduced, most voluntary muscles are inactive and the body becomes less responsive to its surroundings. Far from being a passive shutdown, sleep is an active and highly organised process that is essential for health. During sleep the brain consolidates memories, the body repairs tissues, hormones are released and regulated, and the immune system is strengthened. Long-term lack of sleep is linked to a wide range of problems, including poor concentration, weakened immunity, weight gain, type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and depression.

## How Much Sleep Do People Need?
Sleep needs change across the lifespan. Expert recommendations, such as those from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the US National Sleep Foundation, suggest that adults aged 18 to 60 need at least 7 hours of sleep per night on a regular basis. Teenagers aged 13 to 18 need about 8 to 10 hours, school-age children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours, and newborn babies may sleep 14 to 17 hours a day. Older adults often sleep more lightly and wake more often during the night, but their total sleep need does not fall dramatically. Individual needs vary, and the best sign of adequate sleep is feeling alert and functioning well during the day without relying on caffeine.

## The Stages of Sleep
Sleep is divided into two main types: non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. NREM sleep has three stages. Stage N1 is the light transition between wakefulness and sleep and usually lasts only a few minutes. Stage N2 is a slightly deeper sleep in which body temperature drops and heart rate slows; brain recordings show characteristic bursts of activity called sleep spindles and K-complexes. Adults spend about half of the night in N2. Stage N3, also called deep sleep or slow-wave sleep, is the most restorative stage. It is dominated by slow delta brain waves, it is hardest to wake someone from, and it is when growth hormone is released and much physical repair takes place.

REM sleep is the stage most associated with vivid dreaming. The eyes move rapidly beneath the eyelids, breathing and heart rate become irregular, and the brain is highly active, while most skeletal muscles are temporarily paralysed, which prevents people from acting out their dreams. REM sleep is thought to be important for emotional regulation, learning and memory.

The body cycles through these stages several times each night. One complete cycle of NREM and REM sleep takes about 90 minutes, so a typical night contains four to six cycles. Deep N3 sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, while REM periods become longer in the second half, towards the morning.

## The Body Clock and Melatonin
The timing of sleep is controlled by two processes. The first is the circadian rhythm, an internal clock with a cycle of roughly 24 hours. It is governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a small region of the hypothalamus in the brain, which is set mainly by light entering the eyes. In the evening, as light fades, the pineal gland releases the hormone melatonin, which signals to the body that it is time to sleep. Exposure to bright light, especially blue-rich light from phones, tablets and computer screens, in the late evening can suppress melatonin and delay sleep.

The second process is sleep pressure, also called the homeostatic sleep drive. The longer a person stays awake, the more a chemical called adenosine builds up in the brain, creating a growing need for sleep. Caffeine keeps people alert by blocking adenosine receptors. Because caffeine has a half-life of around five hours in most adults, a cup of coffee in the late afternoon can still interfere with sleep at night.

## Effects of Sleep Deprivation
Even one night of poor sleep impairs attention, reaction time, decision making and mood. Research has found that being awake for around 17 to 19 hours can impair performance to a degree similar to a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05 percent, which is why drowsy driving is so dangerous. Chronic short sleep disturbs the hormones that control appetite, increasing ghrelin, which stimulates hunger, and reducing leptin, which signals fullness, and this contributes to weight gain. Sleep loss also reduces insulin sensitivity, raises blood pressure and weakens the immune response; people who sleep less than about six hours are more likely to catch a cold after exposure to the virus.

## Common Sleep Disorders
Insomnia is difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, despite having the opportunity to sleep. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as the first-line treatment and is generally more effective in the long term than sleeping pills. Obstructive sleep apnoea occurs when the upper airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, causing pauses in breathing, loud snoring and daytime sleepiness; it is commonly treated with a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) machine. Narcolepsy is a neurological disorder causing excessive daytime sleepiness and sudden sleep attacks. Restless legs syndrome causes an uncomfortable urge to move the legs, usually in the evening.

## Habits for Better Sleep
Good sleep hygiene can improve sleep quality for most people. Helpful habits include going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, including weekends; getting exposure to natural daylight in the morning; keeping the bedroom dark, quiet and cool; avoiding caffeine in the afternoon and evening; avoiding heavy meals and alcohol close to bedtime, since alcohol fragments sleep and suppresses REM sleep; limiting screen use for an hour before bed; and taking regular physical exercise, though not too close to bedtime. Short daytime naps of 20 to 30 minutes can boost alertness without causing grogginess. Anyone who regularly struggles with sleep or feels very sleepy during the day should consult a doctor.
